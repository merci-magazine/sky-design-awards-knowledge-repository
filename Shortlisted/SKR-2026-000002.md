## Excerpt

L'hirondelle in Tsukuba combines specialty coffee with a community gallery. Hiro Nakata Atelier uses weathered steel, plaster, and local craftsmanship to create a functional hub for social connection.

## Editorial

Tsukuba is characterized by unique proximity. International researchers and university students live alongside long-established communities, while organized urban districts quickly transition to agricultural landscapes beyond the center. According to Hiro Nakata Atelier, the city lacks informal spaces where these diverse populations can meet without an institutional agenda. The proposed solution is L’hirondelle, a 52.56-square-meter coffee shop designed to address this gap.
Specialty coffee serves as a daily ritual, complemented by a gallery program that extends its public purpose. Exhibitions, activities, and informal encounters can all take place within this compact interior, facilitating connections among visitors from both within and outside the region. The architecture allows for this program to evolve, treating the community not as a fixed audience but as a dynamic entity formed through regular use. The layout is organized around a U-shaped service counter positioned near the center of the space. This open geometry fosters direct interaction between guests and the barista while maintaining a clear working zone behind the counter. Although preparation is visible, the equipment is integrated into the furniture, ensuring that the social exchange of coffee takes precedence over the machinery.
The lower faces of the counter combine dark, weathered metal with warm timber framing. The steel is designed to mature through handling and age, while the timber lends a more domestic scale to the working object. Integrated display niches showcase coffee products within the overall design instead of relying on separate retail fixtures. Above the counter, a suspended timber canopy defines the service area without fully enclosing it. Closely spaced boards create a strong horizontal feature and conceal focused task lighting. A warm perimeter glow elevates the canopy from the pale ceiling, making the counter easily visible from the entrance while maintaining the room’s serene atmosphere.
A curved plaster wall shapes the gallery side of the shop. Its expansive surface bends towards the entrance, and features recessed shelves for coffee, objects, and small works. Both natural and artificial light gently play across the imperfect plaster, giving displayed items the quality of a small installation without reducing the wall to a conventional retail grid. This curve also serves practical purposes: it conceals storage, defines the edge of the gallery, and guides movement around the counter. While the space remains open, the wall creates a distinct room-like condition conducive to display and slower observation.
The corner entrance and extensive glazing provide the compact room with more than one public face. A timber deck extends along the pavement, while additional outdoor seating occupies the side elevation. As a result, the activity inside is visible from multiple directions, reinforcing the shop’s role as an approachable local destination. The lighting is kept small in scale. Simple pendant fittings mark the window seats, recessed sources illuminate the counter, and concealed lighting softens the junction between the canopy and ceiling. These lighting sources are not intended to create a theatrical café image; rather, they support reading, conversation, and the tactile qualities of plaster, timber, and steel.
The colour palette is intentionally limited. Pale plaster and flooring provide a subdued background, while timber adds warmth at the canopy, counter, and bench. Weathered metal adds depth to the service furniture, while leather and small crafted components introduce variation at touchpoints. The space gains character through material aging instead of surface decoration.
Regional products and collaborations extend the interior beyond its walls. Objects created in partnership with local craftspeople are displayed in the gallery and counter niches, connecting the coffee shop to a broader network of production. The programming can seamlessly shift between sales, exhibitions, and community activities without necessitating a separate formal venue.
The deliberate restraint of the setting matters. L’hirondelle does not imitate a community center or burden a small café with symbolic gestures. Its curved wall provides a clear focal point upon arrival, the counter encourages conversation, and the gallery introduces reasons to return that go beyond simply purchasing a drink.
Hiro Nakata Atelier presents coffee culture as a form of modest urban infrastructure. In a city where diversity has developed faster than some social connections, L’hirondelle offers an intimate starting point: local in its craftsmanship, international in its outlook, and adaptable enough to evolve with the diverse people who gather there.
